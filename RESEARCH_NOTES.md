**Factory spec luggage volume:** 7.47 cu ft (~211 L) — the conservative "behind-seats, packable" figure cited in S30 spec sheets ([automobile-catalog](https://www.automobile-catalog.com/make/datsun/z-car_1gen_s30/240z_s30/1972.html), [ultimatespecs](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Datsun/17938/Datsun-S30-240Z.html)).

## Deriving USABLE volume

The bay is a wedge that pinches between the strut towers, so the raw bounding box overstates it badly. Taking it as a trapezoidal prism:

- Average width ≈ (40.5 + 22.5)/2 = **31.5 in**
- Average height (24 front tapering to 8 rear) ≈ **16 in**
- Depth = **42 in**

Raw geometric volume = 31.5 × 16 × 42 = 21,168 in³ = **12.25 cu ft** (theoretical envelope).

That's the air. Real packing — strut intrusions, sloped glass, hard corners you can't fill, no roof rack on a fastback — costs you 35–45%. Apply a 0.62 packing factor → **~7.6 cu ft usable**, which lands right on the factory 7.47 cu ft. 

**USABLE volume for the game: 7.5 cu ft** (use the factory number — it's independently validated by the tape-measure geometry).

## Proposed inventory CAPACITY model

```
USABLE_CUFT        = 7.5      # validated bay volume, seats up
SPARE_WELL_CUFT    = 1.6      # reclaimable if player ditches the spare

# Effective capacity:
#   stock           = 7.5
#   spare removed   = 9.1  (risky — flat tire = run-ending event)
```

**Packing logic:** each item subtracts its packed cu ft; reject the add if it would push total over `USABLE_CUFT`. Apply a small "Tetris bonus" (+0.5 cu ft) only when the load is all soft goods (cooler/bag/tent), since those compress around hard objects.

### Fuel + water budget out to Black Rock Desert (no overpacking)

The S30's own ~16-gal tank gets you there; the cargo bay is your *reserve* for the playa, where there's no gas and no potable water. A non-overpacked loadout:

| Loadout | Contents | Cu ft used | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Balanced (recommended)** | 2× jerry can (10 gal fuel) + 4× 1-gal water + cooler + sleeping bag + tool roll | 1.86 + 0.92 + 1.12 + 0.84 + 0.22 = **4.96** | Leaves ~2.5 cu ft for tent + food. Clean fit. |
| **Fuel-heavy** | 3× jerry can (15 gal) + 3× 1-gal water + bag + tools | 2.79 + 0.69 + 0.84 + 0.22 = **4.54** | Range run. Light on water — flag it. |
| **Survivalist** | 1× jerry (5 gal) + 8× 1-gal water + cooler + tent + bag | 0.93 + 1.84 + 1.12 + 0.62 + 0.84 = **5.35** | Long stay, short range. |

**Carry-out ceiling rule of thumb:** in 7.5 cu ft you can realistically pack **~15 gal of fuel OR ~8 gal of water, but not both at max** — the model caps a sane combined run at roughly **10 gal fuel + 4 gal water** while still fitting shelter and tools. Pulling the spare buys one extra jerry can (5 gal) at the cost of a catastrophic flat-tire risk event — a good in-game tradeoff toggle.

## FACT 1 — Deer-vehicle collision (DVC) risk on rural/mountain two-lane roads at night in NV/CA/AZ/UT, especially November

**Qualitative likelihood (for dice-roll calibration):** A nighttime drive on a two-lane rural/mountain road in the Great Basin / Sierra / Colorado Plateau during November is a *materially elevated, but still per-night-unlikely* event. The conditions stack every known risk multiplier at once — November (rut + fall migration), darkness, low-light two-lane rural geometry, and mountain corridors that funnel deer across the road. Per individual night it remains a low-probability hit; across a multi-day road trip through deer country in November it becomes a plausible "watch for it" hazard. Good design target: low base chance per leg, with a clear multiplier when the leg is *night + rural + November*.

**Timing multipliers (the load-bearing numbers):**
- **November is the single worst month** — highest insurance collision claims nationwide (~12–14 claims per 1,000 insured vehicle-years), roughly **triple** the lowest months; ~25% of the annual DVC total clusters in this window. Driver = the rut (mating season, mid-Oct→early Dec), when bucks drop their caution and dart across roads. (MoneyGeek; The Conversation; KDWP)
- **Night dominates:** ~**76% of DVCs happen at night**. Risk is concentrated at the crepuscular peaks — roughly **8–10 PM** and **6–8 AM**. During dusk/evening, collisions occur up to **14× more often than before sunset**. Deer are crepuscular, and this intensifies during the rut. (MoneyGeek; The Conversation)
- **Rural two-lane roads:** Between **30% and 50%** of drivers who hit wildlife were on **paved rural roads with little traffic and low light** — exactly the game's setting. (MoneyGeek/state data)

**Regional base rates (the four states):**
- Lifetime/annual odds of any animal collision (State Farm-derived): **Nevada ~1 in 834, Arizona ~1 in 509, California ~1 in 456** (Utah not separately listed; nationwide average ~1 in 139). These four are comparatively *low-frequency* states vs. the Midwest/Appalachia — so the per-night base rate should be modest, with the November/night/rural multipliers doing the work. (MoneyGeek)
- **Nevada:** >500 reported animal crashes/yr, ~$19M cost, ~5,032 wild animals killed; **>50% go unreported** (true number higher). Species = mule deer, pronghorn, elk, bighorn. State's #1 hotspot is **US-395 north of Reno** (>50 collisions/yr there alone). (Pew; NDOT)
- **California:** **>48,000 deer killed/yr** on roads (~$200M/yr); mule deer the most-hit large animal. Mountain hotspots concentrate in the **central Sierra — Highways 4, 88, 108**, plus **I-80 / SR-49 / US-50** in the Sacramento–Placerville corridor. (UC Davis Road Ecology Center)
- **Utah/Arizona (migration corridor):** The **Paunsaugunt mule deer herd** migrates **Oct–Nov**, moving south ~36 miles (up to ~78 mi) to winter range along the **Utah–Arizona border**; **US-89 near Kanab** bisects this corridor and is a documented DVC problem spot. This is the cleanest "fall migration crosses the highway at night in November" scenario for the game's geography. (USGS migration data; UDOT)

**One-line scale anchor:** ~1–2.4 million DVCs/yr in the US, ~$1B+ damage, ~200 deaths, ~29,000 serious injuries — i.e., when it happens it's a real, sometimes-fatal event, which justifies high stakes on a *failed* roll. (worldmetrics/III; ARC Solutions)

## FACT 2 — Police checking ID on someone sleeping in a roadside car; flashy show car drawing overnight attention

**Plausibility: high, and legally normal as an *encounter*.** An officer who spots a person asleep in a parked car may approach, knock on the window, ask questions, and **request ID** under the **"community caretaking" / welfare-check doctrine** — a recognized basis for a brief contact when there's a legitimate safety concern (is this person OK, impaired, in distress?). This starts as a **consensual encounter**, so the ask itself needs no suspicion. A graveyard-shift deputy rolling up on a car parked on a rural shoulder at 2 AM and tapping the glass is a textbook, realistic beat. (The Civil Rights Lawyer; Jacksonville/Florida defense blogs; Avvo)

**Where it can escalate (useful for branching outcomes):**
- The welfare check is **supposed to end** once the officer confirms the person needs no help — *unless* they develop **independent reasonable suspicion** of a crime (e.g., signs of impairment, open container, warrants, an in-progress offense). At that point it becomes a detention/investigation. (Jacksonville blog; Florida caretaking analyses)
- **DUI/"sleeping it off" trap:** In many states a person asleep in a parked car can still be arrested for being **in actual physical control** of the vehicle while impaired (keys/engine accessible) — sleeping in the car is *not* automatically safe harbor. (BHW Law, Texas)
- Courts **do** police the limits: a deputy who yanked a still-sleeping person out through an opened door without warning was found to have converted the welfare check into an unconstitutional Fourth Amendment **seizure**. So an aggressive cop overstepping is also realistic — and can be a "the stop was bogus" plot lever. (caretaking case law via the defense blogs)

**Flashy / show car drawing attention — two distinct mechanisms:**
1. **Parked-overnight angle:** A vehicle that **lingers, is parked where it doesn't belong, or sits too long** is exactly what residents and patrols are told to flag; many jurisdictions treat **>72 hours** as abandoned and will run plates or tow. A conspicuous, unfamiliar, eye-catching car parked overnight on a rural shoulder or small-town street is *more* likely to be noticed and reported than a beige sedan — visibility cuts against you here. (Fremont PD; Nolo; neighborhood-watch guidance)
2. **The car itself as a stop magnet:** Independent of parking, a modified/attention-grabbing build invites enforcement — **illegal window tint, loud exhaust (some areas use sound-camera enforcement), neon underglow (banned in WA/VA/PA/MI/IL and others), very loud stereo (FL: audible from 25+ ft = stop), out lights, expired tags, cracked windshield.** A "show car" with underglow or a loud exhaust is a rolling probable-cause buffet. (MotorBiscuit)

**Net for the game:** Both beats are well-grounded. A roadside ID check on a sleeping driver is *plausible and routine* (good for a medium-difficulty social/skill roll, not an auto-fail), and a flashy overnight-parked car **raises the encounter probability** — a clean justification for adding a modifier to the "do you get noticed by a cop tonight?" roll.
